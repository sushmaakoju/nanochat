This video presents the neuroscience framing from my page of October 4, 2026 — how trauma, hate speech, lies, and envy act on the brain. It stays with the concepts from the page, in calm, academic terms. First, the hippocampus. It actively tags and organizes external sensory cues, such as sounds and images — that is encoding. Once sensory cues are encoded, the hippocampus groups similar cues together and stores them as memories — that is consolidation. Then the amygdala and the stress chemistry. The amygdala acts like a filter, capturing and storing emotionally charged or fearful information as memories. High levels of catecholamines impair the mechanisms that control rational thought, while increased output of endogenous opioids and oxytocin can compromise the ability to effectively express emotion. High concentrations of corticosteroids can drain the body of energy, causing it to shut down or freeze. With no intoxication involved, victims experience tonic immobility or dissociation, rather than fighting back. How does this vary under intoxication? A sexual assault can by itself impair basic brain functions and restrict the ability to express emotion or fight back — so under voluntary intoxication, deprivation and recollection become that much more challenging, and the memories and fight-or-freeze responses are significantly different. Inconsistent accounts should not be read as lying. Hate speech has its own neuroscience. Exposure to hate speech deteriorates the neurocognitive mechanisms of the ability to understand others' pain. Research argues that reduced empathy may be caused by dehumanization, not the other way round — and expressing hate discards the responsibility to respect or empathize with a person. Lying is cognitively demanding: it requires significantly more mental energy than truth-telling. The prefrontal cortex suppresses the truth and constructs a false narrative, the anterior cingulate cortex detects the conflict between the lie and the truth, and the amygdala generates fear, anxiety, or guilt. But with repeated dishonesty the brain adapts, and detection normalizes. Being lied to activates the anterior insula, which processes the social pain of betrayal in the same regions associated with physical pain. Envy, meanwhile, activates the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex as social pain — and stronger envy predicted stronger reward activation for schadenfreude. The conclusion from the page: what people in prominent, socially significant roles do impacts many humans neuroscientifically — encoding experiences and their impacts into brain regions, not just into text, AI, and society. This video is dedicated to an inspiring post by Andrej Karpathy — and to the love of languages: controlled, constructed, formal, and everything natural. Controlled natural languages like ASD-STE100, written in Definite Clause Grammar, are designed to translate declarative and interrogative sentences into first-order logic notations — discourse representation structures. Definite Clause Grammar resolves anaphoric references only after the discourse representation structure is constructed; Processable English, or PENG, resolves them during parsing, while the structures are being built. The family is wide. PENG is a controlled natural language. CPL, the Computer Processable Language developed by Boeing, resolves ambiguities using heuristic rules — for prepositional phrase attachment, word sense disambiguation, and semantic role labeling. It accepts ground facts, questions, and rules, using conventional 'if this, then that' with optional AND connectors. Attempto Controlled English, ACE, uses a small set of interpretation rules. On expressivity: E2V is a decidable two-variable fragment of first-order logic — if a formula in that fragment is satisfiable, it is satisfiable in a finite model. In Kuhn's PENS classification of precision, expressiveness, naturalness, and simplicity, ASD-STE is classified P2, E5, N5, S1. Lite NL, based on categorical grammar, has the same expressivity as description logic DL-Lite, a tractable fragment of OWL. ASD-STE100 regulates language for procedural writing — instructions — descriptive writing — giving information — and rules for safety instructions, using the COREL scheme for L-1, L-2, and L-3 constructions. And in my own draft thesis proposal from 2022 and 2023, I was aiming for a subset of natural language that was controlled, yet constructed, to eventually translate to first-order logic. In 2023, large language models could translate to first-order logic — but not deterministically. The page closes with a question I am still asking: is there a controlled natural language, ACE, PENG, or other decidable format of a subset of natural language — for Lean? Question: What are incoherent universals?
Answer: A comical example Sushma developed to verify theories about colour and homonymous keywords. Phrases like “Xs contain ABC” or “Xs have DEF” assert properties universally for two different meanings of X in the same premise — which makes the inference logically inconclusive, and introduces the equivocation fallacy.

Question: What is the bat example?
Answer: A small knowledge base built on the homonymous word “bat”: Bat is a mammal. Bat is used in baseball. Bats have wings. Bats have a barrel. Wings are not barrels. Barrels are not wings. Bats can fly. There is a Brown bat. The query: “Brown bat is a mammal.” Because the word carries two meanings at once, the inference is logically inconclusive — that’s the point.

Question: What is equivocation?
Answer: The logical fallacy of using the same word with two different meanings without distinguishing them. In the bat example: “bats have wings” and “bats have a barrel” both use “bat” — one means the animal, one means the baseball equipment — and the sentence never says which is which. Sushma’s claim is that introducing incoherent universals is equivocation.

Question: What is a homonymous keyword?
Answer: A word with more than one meaning — bat, bank, bark. Both humans and AI, Sushma argues, always ground such a word to one of its meanings to process it. First-order logic does not require that grounding — and that mismatch is what the bat example exposes.

Question: What are the 32 examples?
Answer: Thirty-two examples, generated synthetically from four hand-curated human examples, to test whether the Neutral label in NLI is the same as logically inconclusive. The conclusion she draws: no matter what colour or group-based name is used, the phrase is commonly grounded to one of the two meanings of the keyword — mammal animal, or baseball equipment.

Question: How does trauma affect the brain?
Answer: In Sushma’s conceptual framing: the hippocampus tags and organizes sensory cues (encoding), then groups similar cues into memories (consolidation). The amygdala captures emotionally charged or fearful information. High catecholamines impair rational thought, corticosteroids drain energy, and the body can shut down or freeze — tonic immobility or dissociation, rather than fighting back.

Question: What does hate speech do to the brain?
Answer: Exposure to hate speech deteriorates the neurocognitive mechanisms of understanding others’ pain. Research argues reduced empathy may be caused by dehumanization, not the other way round — and expressing hate discards the responsibility to respect or empathize with a person.

Question: Why is lying hard for the brain?
Answer: Lying is cognitively demanding — it takes significantly more mental energy than truth-telling. The prefrontal cortex suppresses the truth and constructs a false narrative, the anterior cingulate cortex detects the conflict between lie and truth, and the amygdala generates fear, anxiety or guilt. But with repeated dishonesty, the brain adapts and detection normalizes.

Question: What happens in the brain when you are lied to?
Answer: Being lied to activates the anterior insula, which processes the social pain of betrayal — in the same regions associated with physical pain. Betrayal, in other words, hurts like an injury.

Question: What does envy do in the brain?
Answer: Envy activates the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex as social pain — and stronger envy predicted stronger reward activation for schadenfreude, pleasure at another’s misfortune. Sushma’s conclusion: what people in prominent, socially significant roles do impacts many humans neuroscientifically, not just in text or in society.
